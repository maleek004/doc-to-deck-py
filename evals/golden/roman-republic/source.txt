The Roman Republic
Rome was a republic for almost five centuries, from the expulsion of its last king
to the rise of the first emperor. These notes cover how it was governed, how the
plebeians won political rights, the wars with Carthage and why the Republic fell.
1. Founding of the Republic
According to Roman tradition, in 509 BC the Romans drove out their last king,
Tarquin the Proud, and replaced the monarchy with a republic. Power would no longer
belong to one man for life; it was split between elected officials who served for
a fixed term.
2. Magistrates
The highest officials were two consuls, elected every year. Each consul could veto
the other, so neither could rule alone. In an emergency the consuls could appoint a
dictator with supreme power for at most six months. Below the consuls, praetors
ran the courts and censors kept the list of citizens and their property.
3. The Senate and the assemblies
The Senate was made up mostly of former magistrates, who sat in it for life. In
theory it only gave advice, but in practice it controlled finance and foreign
policy. The popular assemblies of citizens elected the magistrates and voted on laws.
4. The Conflict of the Orders
For two centuries the plebeians, the ordinary citizens, struggled against the
patricians, the old aristocratic families, for political rights. The plebeians won
their own officials, the tribunes of the plebs, who could veto acts of magistrates
that harmed plebeians. Around 450 BC the laws were written down for the first time
as the Twelve Tables, so that patrician judges could no longer apply them as they
pleased.

5. The Punic Wars
Rome fought three wars against Carthage, a trading power in North Africa, between
the years 264 and 146 BC. In the Second Punic War the Carthaginian general Hannibal
crossed the Alps with an army and war elephants in 218 BC, and destroyed a much
larger Roman army at Cannae in 216 BC. Rome recovered and won the war. At the end
of the third war, in 146 BC, Rome destroyed the city of Carthage.
6. The fall of the Republic
Conquest brought wealth to the rich and ruin to many small farmers. The brothers
Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, tribunes who proposed giving public land to the poor,
were both killed by their opponents. Generals came to command armies loyal to them
rather than to the state. In 49 BC Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon river with his
army, starting a civil war. He was made dictator for life and was assassinated on
the Ides of March, 44 BC. His adopted heir Octavian defeated his rivals, and in
the year 27 BC he took the name Augustus, becoming the first emperor of Rome.
