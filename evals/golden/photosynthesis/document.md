# Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is how plants, algae and cyanobacteria turn light energy into chemical energy. They take in carbon dioxide and water and make glucose, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The overall equation is 6CO2 + 6H2O + light → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Cellular respiration, which releases the energy stored in glucose, is covered in a separate set of notes.

## Where it happens

In plants, photosynthesis takes place in chloroplasts, mostly in the leaf. A chloroplast contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs called thylakoids, surrounded by a fluid called the stroma. The pigment chlorophyll a, held in the thylakoid membranes, absorbs mostly blue and red light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.

## The light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. Light energy absorbed by photosystem II is used to split water molecules, a step called photolysis. Splitting water releases the oxygen that plants give off, along with protons and electrons. The electrons pass along an electron transport chain to photosystem I. The energy captured along the way is stored in two carrier molecules: ATP and NADPH.

## The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. It uses the ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions to fix carbon dioxide into sugar. The enzyme RuBisCO attaches CO2 to a five-carbon molecule called RuBP. After several steps the cycle produces G3P, a three-carbon sugar that the plant uses to build glucose and other molecules. The Calvin cycle does not use light directly, but it stops within minutes in the dark because it runs out of ATP and NADPH.

## Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis is set by whichever factor is in shortest supply: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. Raising a factor that is not limiting has no effect. Temperature acts through enzymes: the rate rises with warmth up to an optimum, then falls quickly as enzymes such as RuBisCO denature.

## C4 and CAM plants

In hot, dry conditions RuBisCO increasingly binds oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, a wasteful process called photorespiration. C4 plants such as maize and sugarcane avoid it by fixing CO2 in one type of cell and running the Calvin cycle in another: the steps are separated in space. CAM plants such as cacti and pineapple open their stomata only at night to take in CO2, and run the Calvin cycle by day with the stomata closed: the steps are separated in time, which saves water.
