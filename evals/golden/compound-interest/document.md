# Compound interest

With simple interest, only the original amount earns interest. With compound interest, the interest earned is added to the balance and earns interest itself, so the balance grows faster every year.

## The formula

The balance after t years is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where P is the principal (the amount you start with), r is the annual interest rate as a decimal, and n is the number of times interest is compounded per year.

Example: 1,000 invested at 5% a year, compounded once a year, for 10 years grows to 1,000 × 1.05^10 = 1,628.89. With simple interest it would only reach 1,500, because each year adds the same 50.

## Compounding frequency

Compounding more often gives slightly more. The same 1,000 at 5% for 10 years reaches 1,647.01 when compounded monthly (n = 12). The limit, continuous compounding, uses A = Pe^(rt) and gives 1,648.72. The gain from compounding more often shrinks quickly: daily is barely better than monthly.

## The rule of 72

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the interest rate in percent. At 6% a year it doubles in about 72 / 6 = 12 years; at 9%, in about 8. The rule is an approximation that works best for rates between about 4% and 12%.

## APR and APY

The APR (annual percentage rate) is the stated yearly rate before compounding. The APY (annual percentage yield, also called the effective annual rate) includes compounding: APY = (1 + r/n)^n − 1. A loan at 12% APR compounded monthly costs an APY of 12.68%. When comparing savings accounts or loans, compare the APY.

## Inflation

Interest is earned in money, not in purchasing power. The real return is roughly the nominal interest rate minus the inflation rate: a 5% return with 3% inflation is about a 2% real gain.
